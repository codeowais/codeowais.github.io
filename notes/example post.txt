<h1>Pantone Archive was my take on making a color index that felt like flipping through a digital book</h1>
<p>As someone coming from an art-heavy background, the concept of shade cards and color books was familiar to me, but when I tried exploring digital color indexes, the experience felt more like querying a database rather than exploring colors.</p>
<div class="grid-layout" style="grid-template-columns: 1fr 1fr; width: 100%; gap: 20px;">
<a href=https://margaret2.github.io/pantone-colors/><img src='margaret2.github.io.png'></a>
<a href=https://pantone.com/><img src='pantone-connect.jpg'></a>
</div>
<p>Even Pantone’s own search tool felt like it treated color like raw data rather than visual art, completely discarding the familiarity of their iconic postcards — a cornerstone of print culture for generations.</p>
<p>I wanted to change that. I wanted to bridge that gap and build a digital color book that truly felt like one.</p>

<br>

<h1>Chapter 1: A Digital Swatch Book</h1>
<div class="grid-layout" style="grid-template-columns: 1fr 1fr; width: 90%; gap: 20px; padding: 0;">
<img src='pantone-postcards.jpg' style="aspect-ratio: 4/3; object-fit: cover;">
<img src='pantone-wireframe.jpg' style="aspect-ratio: 4/3; object-fit: contain;">
</div>
<p>To translate the feel of the iconic postcard onto a screen, I sat down with a pen to create a sketch of how I felt a modern interpretation of a color book should look like.</p>
<p>I used the Pantone postcards as reference and designed a modern-web friendly element that was responsive to changes, keeping the design cohesive, while also giving the raw shade maximum surface area to visually breathe and anchoring its identification data quietly at the bottom.
</p>

<p>
The result was the UI of Pantone Archive, a marriage between visual beauty and functional tactility, functioning less like an index search and more like an interactive, digital swatch book.</p>

<br>

<h1>Chapter 2: The Architecture of Color</h1>
<p>Behind the visual weight of the postcard grid lies a massive data-parsing operation. A digital color book cannot just look beautiful, it has to function like one as well.</p>
<div class="grid-layout" style="grid-template-columns: 1fr 1fr; width: 90%; gap: 20px; padding: 0;">
<img src="color-swatch.png">
<img src="json-snippet.png">
</div>
<p>Scaling this library required navigating a fundamental hardware limitation. Traditional Pantone catalogs span diverse material mediums, including metallics and coated textiles that rely on physical light reflection. Replicating these specular properties accurately within standard sRGB browser spaces is notoriously inefficient.
<br>
To guarantee absolute visual fidelity on an electronic display, I constrained the scope to the three foundational pillars of digital design: the PMS, FHI, and historic COTY series.</p>
<p>To be able to scale this while preserving functionality over static site serving, I bypassed full-blown databases and structured the color index entirely within a lightweight, nested JSON schema. Each color family acts as a key-value pair, storing unique Pantone codes, production nomenclature, and exact hex values.</p>
<p>The resulting dataset tracks over 4,500 unique, community-validated shades, representing an archive more exhaustive than any of the online tools used during its R&D.</p>

<br>

<h1>Chapter 3: Data, Color and Eye</h1>
<img src="ui-showcase-carousel.png" style="width: 90%; height: auto;">
<p>Pantone Archive started as a critique of how modern interfaces sanitize visual art in favor of raw database querying. By treating code not just as a logic layer, but as a digital printing press, the project reclaims the tactile joy of physical design for the modern web.<br>It is a testament to the idea that utility does not require the sacrifice of soul—and that software can be just as inspiring as the art it catalogs.</p>
